Every week someone asks me which AI tools their business should be paying for. The honest answer is shorter than the question suggests: one subscription, for the right two people, and then a month of actually using it before buying anything else.

This post is the longer version of that answer, written for a small business in Nepal, which changes two things a global listicle ignores: what the tools do with Nepali, and how you pay for them at all.

## Start with one assistant, not a stack

A general assistant, meaning ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, does most of what a small business needs from AI: drafting emails and proposals, summarising documents, translating, rewriting a rough note into a clean message, first drafts of job descriptions and policies, explaining a contract clause, turning a list into a table.

The free tiers of all three are good enough to find out whether this helps your business. Give them to the two people who write and research the most, for two weeks, with one instruction: use it for real work, not for testing. If either of them hits the usage limit on a task they were going to keep doing, pay for that person. If nobody hits a limit, you have learned something cheaper than a subscription.

[Which of the three to pick](/blog/chatgpt-vs-claude-vs-gemini) matters less than committing to one. Switching monthly is how businesses end up with three subscriptions and no habit.

## What is worth paying for, by job

| Job in the business | Tool | Free tier enough? | Paid tier, roughly | My verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Writing, research, summarising, translation | ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini | For trying it, yes | About USD 20 / month per person | **Pay for one**, for the two heaviest users |
| Email, docs and spreadsheets if you use Google Workspace | Gemini inside Workspace | Partly | Bundled into Workspace business plans | Worth it if the team already lives in Gmail and Docs |
| Same, if you use Microsoft 365 | Copilot | Limited | Add-on per user | Only for teams already paying for 365 |
| Social media graphics and simple video | Canva (with its AI features) | Mostly, yes | About USD 10 to 15 / month | Pay only if someone posts several times a week |
| Repetitive processes between apps | n8n | Self-hosted is free | Server at roughly USD 5 to 10 / month, or a hosted plan | **Pay for the server**, once you have [a process worth automating](/blog/is-this-process-worth-automating) |
| Meeting notes | Built-in recording in Google Meet or Zoom, or Otter | Usually, yes | About USD 10 to 20 / month | Rarely worth paying for in a business under twenty people |
| Grammar and tone in English | The assistant you already pay for | Yes | — | Do not buy a separate tool for this |
| Customer replies on WhatsApp | A person, with the assistant drafting | Yes | — | Automate the drafting, not the sending |

Prices move often; check the current page before buying. The verdicts move less.

## How to actually pay from Nepal

This is the part global guides skip, and it decides more purchases than any feature comparison.

Most of these tools bill in dollars to an international card. Nepali banks issue dollar-denominated prepaid or debit cards for online payments under Nepal Rastra Bank rules, with a capped annual allowance that a couple of subscriptions can exhaust. The practical options, in order:

1. **A dollar card from your bank**, used only for the subscriptions that matter. Ask for the annual limit before you plan around it.
2. **Google Play billing**, which several of these tools support on Android and which can be topped up in rupees. This is often the simplest route for Gemini and for ChatGPT's mobile subscription.
3. **A Workspace or 365 plan billed through a Nepali reseller**, which bundles the assistant and invoices in rupees. This suits businesses that want one invoice for accounts.
4. **Self-hosting where possible**, which is why n8n features so often in what I build: a server billed in rupees from a local provider, or a small foreign VPS on the dollar card, replaces a per-seat subscription entirely.

Pick tools you can keep paying for. A subscription that lapses because the card limit ran out in month eight is worse than a free tier used consistently.

## What Nepali language changes

The current generation of models reads Nepali well. Devanagari and Romanised Nepali both work for understanding a customer's message, translating a document, and summarising a report written in Nepali.

Writing is the weaker direction. Model-written Nepali is understandable but often stiff, occasionally wrong in register, and sometimes mixes formal and informal address in one paragraph. For anything a customer will read, draft in English and have a person translate, or let the model produce a first draft in Nepali and edit it. Do not send unedited model-written Nepali to customers. It reads as exactly what it is.

This is also why the automation examples I build for Nepali businesses [keep the reply templates human-written](/blog/ai-automation-examples-nepal) and let the model fill in the fields.

## The four that rarely earn their subscription

I get asked about these regularly. For a small business, the answer is usually no.

**AI website builders.** They produce a site quickly and then charge monthly forever for hosting you do not control, with no way to leave. A [properly built site](/services/web-development) costs more once and then costs almost nothing.

**AI social-media schedulers.** If nobody in the business has a posting habit, the tool does not create one. Canva plus a calendar reminder does the same job for less. If there is a habit, then a scheduler is worth a look, and the [digital marketing](/services/digital-marketing) question is which channels deserve it.

**AI meeting note-takers**, for a team that meets twice a week around one table. Google Meet and Zoom already record and summarise; a separate tool is a second subscription for the same notes.

**Any "AI CRM"** sold to a business that does not have a CRM. The problem is not the absence of AI; it is that enquiries live in three people's WhatsApp. A spreadsheet with [an automation feeding it](/blog/automate-quotation-generation) fixes that for the cost of a server.

## A sensible first three months

| Month | Do | Spend |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Free tiers for the two heaviest writers. One rule about what stays out of the tool. | Nothing |
| 2 | Pay for the one who hit a limit. Write down the three tasks it is used for. | About USD 20 |
| 3 | Look at the three tasks. Pick the one that is repetitive and rule-based, and [ask whether it is worth automating](/blog/is-this-process-worth-automating). | A server, if yes |

By the end of that, you know whether AI is a productivity tool for your business, an automation opportunity, or a distraction. All three are legitimate answers, and finding out cost you a month of attention and twenty dollars.

If the team would use the tools better with a day of hands-on practice, that is what [a training session](/blog/ai-training-syllabus-nepal) is for. If the right next step is a process rather than a tool, the [AI automation service](/services/ai-automation) is the conversation to have. And if you are not sure which, that uncertainty is exactly what [an independent consultation](/services/it-consulting) is for.
