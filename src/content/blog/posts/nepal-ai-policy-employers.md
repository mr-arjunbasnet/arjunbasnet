Most Nepali employers I speak to have heard that Nepal "has an AI policy now". Very few have read what it says, and fewer still know whether it changes anything they have to do on Monday morning.

The short answer is: **the policy sets national direction, but it does not yet create new legal duties for private employers.** The law that already applies to AI at work is an older one, the Privacy Act of 2018. And because rules are coming, the organisations that act now will have the easiest time later.

This post explains the policy in plain terms, what has happened since it was approved, what the existing law already requires, and the five practical steps I would take as an employer. It is a practitioner's summary, not legal advice. For a specific question, ask a lawyer.

## The policy in 150 words

Nepal's Cabinet approved the **National Artificial Intelligence (AI) Policy, 2082** at its meeting on 11 August 2025. It was prepared by the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology and announced publicly a few days later.

According to the *Kathmandu Post*'s report of the policy, its aims include:

- an enabling environment for safe AI;
- institutional, legal and regulatory frameworks;
- ethical, transparent and inclusive use;
- AI skills from school through university;
- research, innovation, startups and AI service exports;
- AI in public services such as agriculture and health;
- protection of citizens' rights, privacy and security against deepfakes, fraud and misuse.

It provides for an **AI Regulation Council** chaired by the communications minister, a **National AI Centre** under the ministry, and a **National AI Index**. It is to be **reviewed every two years**. Commentators have reported a target of training **at least 5,000 qualified AI specialists**, though no timeline has been published. It names priority sectors including IT, health, education, energy, transport, tourism, industry and agriculture.

## What has happened since

| Commitment | Status as of October 2026 | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| National AI Centre | Inaugurated on 10 November 2025 by Communications Minister Jagadish Kharel; its stated role is policy facilitation for firms delivering services through AI (*Rising Nepal*) | A government point of contact for AI businesses now exists |
| AI Regulation Council | The minister said at the same event that a council had been formed; opinion pieces in April 2026 were still calling for it to be set up with independent experts | Treat its membership and working rules as unconfirmed |
| AI Centres of Excellence | The ministry began setting one up with universities in November 2025 (*ekantipur*) | Watch for university partnerships and research programmes |
| AI law | None yet. AI regulation bills are on the government's list of 151 bills planned for FY 2026-27 (*Kathmandu Post*, July 2026); an umbrella science and technology bill covering AI was at the concept stage (*Techpana*, July 2026) | No AI-specific duties today; expect them |
| Budget | The FY 2083/84 budget (29 May 2026) announced the country's first sovereign AI compute centre at Syuchatar, Kathmandu, with concessional compute for AI startups | The state is investing in AI, not restricting it |
| School curriculum | A Grade 1–12 revision with a dedicated AI and ICT committee is under way (*Kathmandu Post*, September 2026) | Future hires will arrive with some AI education |

Critics have pointed to real gaps: the policy names **no budget** and **no implementation dates**, and a ministry official acknowledged it does not yet designate a focal point for reporting AI-related issues. None of that changes what an employer should do. It does mean waiting for detailed rules before acting would be a long wait.

## The law that already applies: the Privacy Act, 2075

While the AI policy is mostly direction, Nepal's **Privacy Act, 2075 (2018)** is law today, and it applies directly to how staff use AI tools. The sections that matter most:

| Section | What it says (paraphrased) | The AI risk it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 12(2) | Personal data may be collected only with consent | Feeding customer or staff data into a tool for a new purpose |
| 12(3) | Data may be used only for the purpose it was collected for | Using HR or client records to "try something" in a chatbot |
| 12(4) | A person's health, income, **employment**, family, biometric or signature information may not be disclosed without consent | Pasting appraisal notes, salary sheets or medical leave records into a public AI tool |
| 26 | No use of personal data without consent | Building AI summaries of named individuals |
| 29 | Penalties of up to three years' imprisonment, a fine of up to Rs 30,000, or both | Why this is not a theoretical concern |

Data handled by a public body carries further duties under section 25. The Act is supported by the Individual Privacy Regulation, 2077.

The practical point is simple. **The riskiest AI behaviour at work in Nepal is already covered by existing law**: an employee pasting someone's employment, health or salary details into a consumer AI account. In my experience this happens constantly, usually with good intentions and no awareness of the rule. In a recent pre-training survey of 23 Nepali team leaders, [only 2 were clearly sure what must never be put into an AI tool](/blog/ai-training-pre-assessment-nepal).

## What the policy does not do yet

To avoid over-reading it:

- It does **not** ban or license any AI tool.
- It does **not** require private companies to register AI use, appoint an AI officer, or train staff.
- It does **not** set rules for AI in hiring, credit or other automated decisions.
- It is **almost silent on education**, as the lead researcher of a 2026 study of Nepali teachers put it. I covered that in [AI training for teachers in Nepal](/blog/ai-training-teachers-nepal).

Some of this will change. The direction of travel is clear: standards on data, algorithms, transparency and accountability, and protection against deepfakes and misuse.

## Five things an employer should do now

None of these requires waiting for an AI act, and each will make future compliance easier.

### 1. Write a one-page AI-use policy

One page, in plain language, covering what AI may be used for, what it may not, and what must never be pasted in. A policy that nobody can remember is worse than a short one people actually follow. The data-class list in step 3 is the core of it.

### 2. Decide approved tools and account types

Choose which tools staff may use for work, and whether that means free personal accounts, a paid team plan, or your company's Google or Microsoft workspace. A company workspace lets you control data settings centrally; personal accounts do not. The cost comparison is in [AI tools for small businesses in Nepal](/blog/ai-tools-small-business-nepal).

### 3. Define data classes

Three classes are usually enough:

| Class | Examples | AI rule |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Published prices, website copy, public notices | Any approved tool |
| Internal | Process documents, draft reports with no personal data | Approved company accounts only |
| Confidential | Staff records, salaries, health, client personal data, contracts | Not in general AI tools without explicit approval and a lawful basis |

### 4. Train staff and keep a record

Training is where a policy becomes behaviour. Keep a simple record of who was trained, when, and on what. If AI rules tighten, evidence that staff were trained on safe use is the kind of thing a regulator, client or auditor will ask for. A practical format is in [AI training for employees in Nepal](/blog/ai-training-syllabus-nepal).

### 5. Name an owner

Give one person responsibility for AI use: keeping the policy current, approving new tools, and fielding questions. In a small company that is often the owner or the operations lead. It does not need a new job title, just a name.

## Where this leaves Nepali organisations

The policy is good news for anyone building with AI. The state wants adoption, skills and investment, and it is putting money into compute. The risk is not that AI will be banned. It is that organisations adopt it with no rules, and the first regulation or the first data incident finds them unprepared.

The organisations in the best position will have three things in writing: a short policy, an approved-tools decision, and a training record.
