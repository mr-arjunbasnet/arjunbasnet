Nepali teachers did not wait for permission. A study published this year found that three in four of them already use generative AI to prepare lessons, simplify concepts, translate terms, make worksheets and assess written work. Most taught themselves. Few have had any training, and there is no national rulebook to follow.

That changes what **AI training for teachers in Nepal** should be. The question is no longer "should teachers use AI?" It is "how do we help teachers who already use it do so safely, accurately and in a way that still makes students think?"

This post covers what the research found, what self-taught use tends to miss, what a teacher workshop should actually include, and a one-page outline for a school AI policy that you can adapt.

## What the 2026 study found

In October 2026 the *Kathmandu Post* published a column by Bhogendra Lamichhane of the British Council Nepal. It summarised a joint study by the **Ministry of Education and Sports and the British Council**. The figures below come from that column and from an August 2026 interview with the study's research lead, Dr Rhonda Zelezny-Green, in *ekantipur*. The full report was described as a draft, so individual figures may still change.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Teachers surveyed | 815, across all seven provinces |
| Had already used generative AI | 76% |
| Male teachers who had used AI | 80.5% |
| Female teachers who had used AI | 71.3% |
| Teachers aged 30 or under using AI | 94% (as reported by the research lead) |
| Teachers aged 50 or over using AI | 54% (as reported by the research lead) |
| How most learned | On their own, "often quietly and without formal guidance" |

The tools named were ChatGPT, Gemini, Google Lens and Google Translate. The uses were practical and everyday: finding information, lesson preparation, simplifying concepts, translating terms, creating worksheets, assessing written work, preparing administrative documents, and generating audio and images.

Two findings matter most for anyone planning training.

**Age is the divide, not geography.** In her *ekantipur* interview, the research lead said urban–rural and ethnic differences were small, while the gap between younger and older teachers was large. Training that assumes everyone is a beginner will bore the under-30s; training that assumes fluency will lose the over-50s. Mixed groups need pairing, not a single pace.

**Training, not infrastructure, was the biggest barrier.** Teachers said they did not know where to get training, and that none had been arranged for them, so they were using AI almost blindly. The study also named practical obstacles: household and caring responsibilities eating into female teachers' time, training offered at the wrong time, place or format, and weak connectivity.

## What is already happening

There is training activity, but it is small and scattered:

- **NAST** ran a full-day trial training for science teachers on ChatGPT, Gemini and DeepSeek, focused on teaching materials and model question papers. Teachers from Chandragiri and Tokha took part, and the stated plan is a standard format with nationwide rollout where possible (*CollegeNP*, November 2025).
- **The Rotary Club of Yala's AI4PEN project** began a 40-hour, ten-week programme for 25 teachers at a public secondary school in Godawari, Lalitpur, in August 2026.
- **The Curriculum Development Centre** is revising the Grade 1–12 curriculum. A task force formed in April 2026 has twelve thematic committees, one of them on AI and ICT (*Kathmandu Post*, September 2026). No AI syllabus had been published at the time of writing.

What does not yet exist, as far as I could find, is **published national guidance on AI use in schools or exams** from the ministry, the CDC or the NEB. The research lead noted that even the 2025 National AI Policy is almost silent on education. Until guidance arrives, each school is setting its own rules, or none.

## What self-taught use misses

Teachers who taught themselves AI are usually good at the parts they use daily. The gaps are predictable, and they are exactly where training earns its keep.

| Classroom task | Safe, useful AI use | Where the line is |
|---|---|---|
| Lesson planning | Drafting an outline, generating examples, varying difficulty | The teacher checks every fact and decides the sequence |
| Worksheets and questions | Generating practice questions at several levels | Answers are verified before use; no copying exam papers into AI |
| Explaining a concept | Producing a simpler explanation or an analogy | Checked for accuracy and for cultural fit in a Nepali classroom |
| Translation (English ↔ Nepali) | First drafts of terms and passages | Every technical term checked; AI Nepali is weaker than AI English |
| Marking written work | Suggesting feedback on anonymised samples | No student names or identifiable work pasted into public tools; grades stay the teacher's decision |
| Admin and reports | Drafting circulars, minutes, notices | No personal data about students, parents or staff |
| Student use | Brainstorming, feedback on drafts, explaining mistakes | Declared, age-appropriate, and never a substitute for the student's own thinking |

The study itself flagged the same risks: output that is false or culturally insensitive, students skipping the thinking, deepfakes, and privacy. Every one of those is teachable in a morning.

## What a teacher AI workshop should cover

This is the half-day format I would recommend for a school, for 15 to 30 teachers. It assumes most of the room already uses AI, which the study says is likely.

| Time | Block | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 30 min | Where we are: a quick show of hands on current use, and the school's concerns | The session pitches at the right level |
| 45 min | Safe use: what must never go into an AI tool (student names, marks, health or family details, exam papers) | Every teacher can name the school's red lines |
| 45 min | Checking output: catching errors, outdated facts, cultural misfit and weak Nepali | A checking routine each teacher will actually use |
| 15 min | Break | |
| 45 min | Hands-on: each teacher builds one real resource for next week's lesson | Something useful leaves the room |
| 30 min | Assessment: designing tasks AI cannot simply complete (process, oral, in-class, local context) | Two redesigned assignments per department |
| 30 min | The school AI policy: agree the draft below | A policy the staff helped write |

The one-day employee version, with a longer hands-on block, is in [AI training for employees in Nepal](/blog/ai-training-syllabus-nepal). The school version differs in two ways: more time on assessment, and the policy is agreed in the room.

The study's most useful recommendation is **mentored classroom practice** after the workshop. A single session starts the change. A follow-up a month later, in which teachers bring what worked and what did not, is what makes it stick.

## A one-page school AI policy outline

No national template exists yet, so here is the outline I suggest schools start from. Keep it to one page and review it each term.

1. **Purpose.** One sentence: AI may support teaching and learning, but it does not replace a teacher's judgement or a student's thinking.
2. **Approved tools by grade.** Name them, and respect each tool's age rule. ChatGPT requires users to be at least 13, with parental permission under 18. Claude's consumer terms require users to be 18. Gemini for under-13s works only through a parent-managed Google account. Check the current terms each year, because they change.
3. **Teachers may use AI for:** planning, resources, differentiation, translation drafts and admin, with output checked before use.
4. **Teachers must never paste in:** student names or marks, personal or family information, health details, or unreleased exam papers.
5. **Students may use AI for:** brainstorming, explanations and feedback on drafts, if the teacher allows it for that task.
6. **Students must declare AI help.** A single line on the work: what tool, for what. Undeclared use is treated like any other academic dishonesty.
7. **Assessment.** Each department marks which tasks are AI-allowed, AI-assisted or AI-free.
8. **Parents.** A short note home explaining the policy, in Nepali and English.
9. **Owner and review date.** A named person, and a date each term to revise the policy.

UNESCO's 2023 guidance on generative AI in education is a useful reference behind several of these points. It recommends protecting data privacy, validating tools before use, and setting an age limit for students' independent use of generative AI. On the age limit, UNESCO suggested considering 13 for classroom use. UNESCO's 2024 AI competency framework for teachers is also a sound structure for a longer programme. It sets out fifteen competencies across five dimensions, at three levels: acquire, deepen and create.

## What to tell parents

Parents are already hearing about AI from their children, and often from news stories about cheating. A short note helps:

- the school is teaching students to use AI **responsibly**, not banning it;
- students must **declare** AI help on their work;
- teachers **do not** put children's personal information into AI tools;
- the school will **review** the policy every term and welcomes questions.

For older students' own use of AI, the companion guide on [AI tools for students in Nepal](/blog/ai-tools-for-students-nepal) sets out what helps and where the academic line sits.

## When a school does not need outside training

Be honest about this before booking anyone, including me:

- **If a few teachers on your staff are confident AI users**, a well-run internal session led by them, using the policy outline above, may be enough to start.
- **If the school has not decided which tools students may use**, decide that first. Training on tools you then restrict wastes the day.
- **If the real problem is connectivity or devices**, fix that first. The study names connectivity as a genuine barrier, and no workshop solves it.

When a school does want outside help, it is usually for three things: a structured session that covers safe use and assessment properly, a facilitator for the policy discussion, and a follow-up a month later. That is the format on the [AI training for schools in Nepal](/ai-training-schools-nepal) page, and the wider programme options are on [AI training in Nepal](/ai-training-nepal).
