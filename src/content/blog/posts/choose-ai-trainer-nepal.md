Most organisations in Nepal book AI training the way they book a venue: they find someone available, agree a date and a fee, and hope for the best. Then the day happens, people enjoy it, and a month later nothing has changed.

The difference between a useful AI trainer and an expensive slideshow is visible before you book, if you ask the right questions. These are the nine I would ask if I were the HR or L&D manager on the other side of the table. I am an [AI trainer in Nepal](/ai-trainer-nepal) myself, so read this knowing that. I have tried to write the questions so that they would also catch me out if my answers were weak.

If you are still deciding whether you need a trainer at all, skip to [when you should not hire one](#when-you-should-not-hire-an-ai-trainer).

## The nine questions

### 1. "Will the session use our real tasks?"

This is the most important question, and the answer tells you almost everything.

**A good answer:** "Yes. Before the session I'll ask what your team spends its week on, and each participant brings one real task." The day is then built around your accounts process, your customer emails, your reports.

**A red flag:** "We have a standard programme." Standard programmes use invented examples, such as writing a poem or planning a holiday. People are entertained and forget it by Friday. Retention is much better when people work on their own material.

### 2. "Do you run a pre-assessment?"

A short form before the day tells the trainer what the room already knows. Without one, the session pitches at the wrong level: it either explains prompting to people who use AI every day, or skips the basics for people who have never logged in.

When I ran one recently for 23 team leaders in Kathmandu, 21 already used AI every day and all 23 had written detailed prompts. A standard introductory workshop would have wasted their morning. The full results are in [what 23 Nepali team leaders told us before their AI training](/blog/ai-training-pre-assessment-nepal).

**Red flag:** no pre-assessment, or a form that asks only name and department.

### 3. "Which tools, and on which accounts?"

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot are all reasonable choices. What matters is that the trainer names one, and names the **account type**: free consumer accounts, a paid team plan, or your company's Google or Microsoft workspace.

That decision sets what data participants may use on the day. A trainer who encourages your staff to paste client files into free personal accounts during the exercise has just taught them the wrong lesson. The honest comparison between the tools is in [ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini](/blog/chatgpt-vs-claude-vs-gemini).

**Good answer:** "Let's agree the tool and account type with you first. Exercises use only data your rules allow."

### 4. "What will participants be able to do at the end?"

Ask for outcomes as verbs, not topics. "Understand generative AI" is a topic. "Write a reusable prompt for our weekly report and check its output" is an outcome.

**Good answer:** three to five concrete abilities, each tied to work your team does.

**Red flag:** a list of modules with no outcomes, or outcomes like "be AI-ready".

### 5. "What do you cover on safe use?"

Every AI training programme in Nepal needs a block on what must never go into an AI tool: client personal data, staff records, contracts, passwords, unreleased financials. It should also cover how to check an answer before acting on it.

This is not optional caution. In most Nepali organisations staff adopted AI before any company rule existed, and most are unsure where the line is. A trainer should leave you with a one-page rule your team can follow, not just a warning.

**Red flag:** "We'll touch on ethics at the end." Safe use should be a block of its own, early in the day, before the hands-on work.

### 6. "How many people per session, and for how long?"

Hands-on AI training stops working beyond roughly 30 participants. The trainer can no longer see screens or help pairs who are stuck. A room of 80 is a seminar, and seminars raise awareness but rarely change how people work.

| Format | Group size | Length | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seminar or talk | 30–200 | 1–2 hours | Awareness, leadership buy-in, launching a programme |
| Workshop | 8–30 | 1 day or 2 half-days | Changing how a team works day to day |
| Intensive | 8–20 | 3 days | Teams that will automate processes or build shared assistants |
| Leadership session | 5–15 | Half a day | Policy, tool and account decisions, measuring adoption |

**Good answer:** a trainer who asks about your goal before agreeing a format, and who will split a large team into two sessions.

### 7. "Will it be in Nepali, English or both?"

Many teams learn better in Nepali, with English tool names. Ask too whether the trainer covers **checking AI output in Nepali**. The models are noticeably weaker in Nepali than English, and Nepali teams translate between the two all the time: customer messages, notices, scripts, summaries. A good session teaches a routine for catching a wrong figure, name or tone.

### 8. "What happens four weeks later?"

The real measure of AI training is not a feedback form on the day. It is how many participants still use something they built, a month later.

**Good answer:** a short follow-up: a check-in call, a survey, or a review of the workflows people built, with a number reported back to you.

**Red flag:** the engagement ends when the trainer leaves the room. A certificate is not an outcome either; it proves attendance.

### 9. "What can you show me from a past session?"

Ask for something concrete: a sample syllabus, a pre-assessment form, the rule sheet a previous team left with, or a reference you can call. A trainer with real delivery behind them can show the material without naming the client.

Mine is public: the [one-day AI workshop syllabus](/blog/ai-training-syllabus-nepal) I use for employee training is published in full.

**Red flag:** only photos of a full hall, or only a list of logos with nothing behind them.

## A quick scoring sheet

Use this to compare proposals side by side.

| Question | Strong answer | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Real tasks? | Built around your team's work | "Standard programme" |
| Pre-assessment? | Yes, with usage and task questions | None |
| Tools and accounts? | Named, agreed with you, within your data rules | Whatever people have on their phones |
| Outcomes? | 3–5 abilities, written as verbs | Topics only, or "AI-ready" |
| Safe use? | Its own block, plus a one-page rule | "A few slides at the end" |
| Group size? | ≤ 30 for hands-on work; splits large teams | 80 in one room, called a workshop |
| Language? | Your choice, including checking Nepali output | English only, no discussion |
| Follow-up? | A 4-week check with a number | Ends on the day |
| Evidence? | Syllabus, sample materials, a reference | Hall photos and logos |

A proposal that scores well on the first five is usually worth taking forward. One that fails question 1 or question 5 is not, whatever the price.

## What a fair proposal contains

When the trainer comes back with a written proposal, it should state:

- **The audience and group size**, and what happens if more people sign up.
- **The format and length**, with an hour-by-hour outline.
- **The tool and account type** used in exercises.
- **The outcomes**, as abilities.
- **The pre-assessment and follow-up**, with dates.
- **What you need to provide**: a room, screens, laptops, Wi-Fi that works, accounts set up in advance.
- **The fee and what it includes**: preparation, materials, follow-up, travel outside Kathmandu.

If a proposal is vague on these, ask. Read it the way you would read a software quote; the same habits apply, and I covered them in [how to evaluate a vendor's proposal](/blog/evaluate-software-vendor-proposal).

## When you should not hire an AI trainer

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not need one yet.

- **If only two or three individuals need general AI literacy**, a free or low-cost online course is better value. I compared the options in [AI courses in Nepal](/blog/ai-courses-nepal-compared).
- **If nobody has decided which tool or account the organisation will use**, settle that first. Training people on a tool you then do not pay for is wasted.
- **If leadership is not going to use AI themselves**, start with a short leadership session, not a staff workshop. Teams copy what their managers do, not what a trainer says.
- **If what you actually need is a process automated**, you may need [automation work](/services/ai-automation) more than training. Teaching twenty people to do something by hand with AI, when it could run on its own, is the wrong fix.

## The short version

Book the trainer who asks the most questions about your team before quoting. Insist on real tasks, a named tool and account, a safe-use block, a group small enough to be hands-on, and a check four weeks later. Everything else is detail.
