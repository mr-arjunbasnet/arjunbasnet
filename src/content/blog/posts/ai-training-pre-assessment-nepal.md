Before any AI training I run, participants fill in a short form. It takes ten minutes, and it is the most useful ten minutes of the whole engagement, because it tells me what the room already knows. Without it, a trainer either explains prompting to people who do it every day or skips the basics for people who have never opened ChatGPT. Either way the day is wasted.

In late September 2026, twenty-three team leaders at a Kathmandu company answered that form before their programme. Sixteen lead sales teams; seven lead other functions. The company is not named here, and nothing below identifies a person. What their answers show is worth publishing anyway, because I think it describes a large share of Nepali workplaces right now: **AI is already in daily use, and almost none of the rules around it exist.**

This is a small sample from one organisation. I will say that again where it matters. But it is real data from people who use these tools at work, and that is rarer than it should be in conversations about [AI training in Nepal](/ai-training-nepal).

## The headline: daily use, no rules

Twenty-one of the 23 use ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Copilot **every day**. The other two use them a few times a week. Nobody was a beginner.

That alone changes what a training day should be. The usual opening of a corporate AI workshop, which covers what a large language model is, how to open an account and what a prompt looks like, would have been an hour of boredom for this room.

Now set that against the question that matters most for an employer:

**Two people out of twenty-three** were confident they knew what must never be pasted into an AI tool. Seventeen thought so but were not sure. Four said no.

These are team leaders. They review call recordings, read client emails, track individual sales figures and write feedback about named staff. That is exactly the material that should not go into a consumer chatbot without someone having decided it can. And when eight of them listed what stops them using AI more, the answer was *"I'm not sure what is safe to share."*

This is not carelessness. Nobody told them. In my experience, that is the normal state of a Nepali organisation in 2026: staff adopted AI on their own, ahead of any policy, because it was free and it helped.

All 23 could log in to free ChatGPT. Most also had free Gemini and free Claude. Only two had a paid or company account of anything, and in both cases it was Gemini through a Google account. Twenty of the 23 said their team runs on Gmail and Google Calendar, so a company Google workspace already existed; its AI features simply were not what people reached for.

A free consumer account is not wrong in itself. The problem is that the organisation has no say over it: it cannot set data retention, cannot switch off training on conversations centrally, cannot see what is being used, and cannot take the history back when someone leaves. For a team handling client data, that decision should be made once, by the company, in writing, rather than twenty-three times by individuals. I set out the options and costs in [AI tools for small businesses in Nepal](/blog/ai-tools-small-business-nepal).

Every single participant had already given an AI a detailed prompt with context and the output format they wanted. That is the skill most "prompt engineering" courses spend a day teaching. This group did not need it.

Read down the chart, though, and the shape is a cliff. The first six rows are things you do in a chat window: ask, summarise, draft, translate. Then it falls away. Eight had saved instructions to reuse, such as a custom GPT, a Gem or a Claude Project. Four had set anything to run on a schedule. Three had connected AI to their email, calendar or sheets. **One** had built an automation between two apps.

So the gap in this room was not "how do I talk to AI". It was "how do I stop doing the same thing by hand every week". That is a different skill. It is closer to [AI automation](/services/ai-automation) than to prompting, and it is where the hours actually come back. I wrote about where to start in [processes worth automating first](/blog/processes-worth-automating-first).

### Nepali translation is already a daily use

Fifteen of the 23 had used AI to translate between English and Nepali. In a Nepali workplace that is a core use, not an edge case: customer messages, notices, scripts and summaries move between the two languages constantly.

It also carries a specific risk. The models are noticeably weaker in Nepali than in English. They are fluent enough to sound right and loose enough to change a figure, a name or a tone. Any AI training in Nepal that skips "how to check Nepali output before it goes to a customer" is missing something these participants do every week. One participant also listed "hard to explain what I want in English" as a barrier, which is a reminder that the session language matters too.

## Where their time actually goes

Asked which tasks take most of their week (up to three each), the team leaders answered:

| Task | Team leaders who picked it (of 23) | Can AI help today? |
|---|---|---|
| Call quality or QA reviews | 12 | Partly. A transcript can be summarised and scored against a rubric, but the judgement and the feedback conversation stay human. |
| Messages and follow-ups with my team | 11 | Yes, for drafting and for turning a meeting into named actions. |
| Coaching, 1:1s and feedback | 8 | Preparation only: structuring notes and spotting patterns across weeks. Not the conversation. |
| Emails and follow-ups with clients | 8 | Yes, with a human sending every message. |
| Meetings and meeting notes | 7 | Yes. This is the most mature use of the lot. |
| Tracking targets and team numbers | 6 | Yes, if the numbers already live in a sheet AI can read. |
| Daily and weekly reports | 5 | Yes. This is the best candidate for a scheduled, automated workflow. |

Two things stand out. First, almost every item is communication or review work, which is exactly where AI assistants are strongest. Second, the most time-consuming items, QA reviews and coaching, involve personal data about named staff and recorded customers. The task with the most potential is also the one with the strictest data rules. That is why the safety question in the first chart is not a side issue.

When I asked what they wished AI could do for them, the free-text answers were specific: review sales calls and summarise the insights, analyse the team's weekly performance, automate routine emails, schedule 1:1s. One put it better than I could: the challenge "is not getting information; it is converting the information I already have" into action.

## What they wanted to leave able to do

Each participant picked up to two outcomes:

- **Find the parts of my work AI can speed up**: 10
- **Write better prompts and get better answers**: 9
- **Use AI safely with client and team information**: 8
- **Schedule and automate tasks that repeat**: 7
- **Plan projects with AI** (goals, timeline, tasks, owners): 7
- **Build an AI workflow for my team**: 5

Nine still wanted better prompts, even though all 23 already wrote detailed ones. That is honest self-assessment: people who use AI daily know their answers could be better. Twelve said nothing stops them using AI more because they already use it a lot. Eight said the answers are not good enough. Both groups need the same thing, which is technique applied to their own work rather than generic examples.

## What this means for designing an AI training day

Here is how I read the survey, and how I think any organisation in Nepal should read its own.

| If your pre-assessment shows… | Then the training day should… |
|---|---|
| Most people use AI daily | Skip the introduction. Start from their own tasks in the first hour. |
| Few are sure what is unsafe to share | Spend a full block on data classes: what is public, internal or confidential, with examples from their own work. Leave with a one-page rule. |
| Everyone is on free accounts | Put the workspace decision to management before the session, not after. |
| Many translate English ↔ Nepali | Teach a checking routine for Nepali output: figures, names, tone, and a back-translation spot check. |
| Almost nobody has automated anything | Build one small scheduled workflow per participant, such as a weekly report or a meeting-notes summary. |
| The biggest time sinks involve personal data | Handle those tasks last, once the rules are clear, and only in a tool the company has approved. |

The general version of the syllabus is in [AI training for employees in Nepal](/blog/ai-training-syllabus-nepal), and the change-management side is in [how to introduce AI to your team](/blog/introduce-ai-to-your-team). The survey's job is to tell you which half of that syllabus your team actually needs.

## The limits of this data

To be plain about what this is and is not:

- **It is 23 people at one company.** They were mostly sales team leaders, and they had already been chosen for an AI programme. A random sample of Nepali office workers would almost certainly show lower daily use.
- **It is self-reported.** "Every day" and "I think so" are people's own descriptions.
- **It was collected on 30 September and 1 October 2026,** before training. The company is not named and nothing here identifies a person.

I would still bet the shape holds widely: adoption ahead of policy, free accounts ahead of company ones, and chat-window use ahead of real automation. If your organisation is in that position, the fix is not a ban. It is a short written rule, an account decision, and a training day built around what your people already do.
