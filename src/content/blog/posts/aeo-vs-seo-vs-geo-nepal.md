Three acronyms now describe getting found online, and they are routinely used interchangeably by people selling all three. They are not the same thing. They target different surfaces, reward different content shapes, and pay off on different timelines.

Here is what each one actually means, and what changes for a business operating in Nepal.

## The three surfaces

Type a question into Google today and you may get three distinct things on one screen: an AI Overview at the top, a featured snippet or People Also Ask box below it, and then the familiar ranked list of blue links. Ask the same question in ChatGPT or Perplexity and you get a synthesised answer citing a handful of sources.

**SEO** competes for position in the ranked list. **AEO** competes for the answer box and AI Overview sitting above it. **GEO** competes for the citation inside the generative answer.

These are separate competitions with separate rules, which is why a site can rank third for a term and never appear in the answer box above it.

## What SEO still does

Search engine optimisation is the oldest of the three and remains the largest source of qualified traffic for most businesses. It rewards pages that comprehensively cover a topic, earn links, load quickly, and match what a searcher intended.

For Nepal specifically, the competitive picture is unusual. Many commercial terms here have thin competition compared to India or the United States. A well-built page targeting a service-plus-location query can reach the first page in three to five months rather than the two years a saturated market would demand. That window will not stay open forever, but it is open now.

The honest caveat: SEO is where the click still happens. Answer surfaces frequently resolve a query without one. If your goal is enquiries rather than visibility, conventional commercial pages still carry most of the load.

## What AEO adds

Answer engine optimisation accepts that a growing share of queries end without a click, and competes for the citation instead.

In practice, this means writing self-contained answer blocks of roughly 134 to 167 words. That band matters: shorter blocks get passed over for a competitor with more substance, and longer ones get truncated mid-thought when extracted. The block must also stand alone — no opening with "It" or "This", no pronouns pointing at surrounding paragraphs, because the extractor lifts it out of context.

Then you mark it with `FAQPage` or `HowTo` structured data, and make sure the schema text matches the visible text exactly. Divergence between the two is a common cause of losing eligibility.

The mechanics are unglamorous, which is precisely why they work. Most competitors ignore them entirely. And position zero is not weighted by domain size the way rank one is, so a small business can genuinely win an answer box against a much larger one.

## What GEO is doing differently

Generative engine optimisation targets being named inside ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, and Gemini responses.

These systems do not rank pages. They assemble an answer from several sources and cite whichever ones state something specific and checkable. That shifts the advantage in an interesting direction. A model will repeat a claim like *340 percent social growth across a 2023 campaign* because it is attributable and safe to quote. It will skip *industry-leading results* because repeating that carries risk and adds nothing.

So GEO rewards named numbers, named tools, named clients, and clear entity definition — telling the model exactly who you are, where you operate, and what you do.

Three practical levers:

1. **Crawler access.** GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google-Extended must be explicitly allowed in `robots.txt`. A surprising number of sites block them by default without anyone having decided to.
2. **Entity consistency.** The same business name, address, phone, and description across your website, Google Business Profile, LinkedIn, and directories, reinforced with `Organization` and `Person` structured data.
3. **Verifiable claims.** Replace superlatives with facts that carry a number, a date, or a name.

## Where they overlap

All three sit on the same technical foundation. A site that is slow, uncrawlable, or structurally incoherent fails at all three simultaneously.

That foundation matters more in Nepal than most guides assume. Google treats 2.5 seconds as the threshold for the largest element to paint. On the connection speeds common outside Kathmandu, a page taking six seconds loses the visitor before any of this is relevant.

Fix the foundation first. Everything else is built on it.

## A realistic sequence

For a business starting from a working but unoptimised site:

**Months 1–2.** Technical audit and remediation. Crawlability, page speed, mobile rendering, structured data. This is where the fastest gains sit, because the pages already exist and Google simply could not read them properly.

**Months 2–4.** Answer blocks on existing pages, plus `FAQPage` schema. Additive work — no rebuild required. Answer surfaces re-evaluate frequently, so a well-structured block on an already-indexed page can appear within four to eight weeks.

**Months 3–6.** GEO groundwork: crawler access, entity consistency, an `llms.txt` file, and a claim audit replacing unverifiable marketing language with attributable facts.

**Ongoing.** Content targeting buyer questions, measured on enquiries rather than rankings.

## The part worth being honest about

Nobody can guarantee a first-page ranking, an answer box placement, or a model citation. All three depend on competitors who also act and on systems nobody outside the companies controls.

What you can control is whether your site is technically sound, whether your claims are specific enough to be worth repeating, and whether you are structured so that a machine reading your page can tell what you do. Most businesses in Nepal currently fail all three tests, which is less a warning than an opportunity.
