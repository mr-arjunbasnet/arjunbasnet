Most advice about AI tools for students is written by people who have never sat
a Tribhuvan University exam. It tends to be a list of twenty apps, none of which
addresses the actual question, which is: what can I use without damaging my own
learning or getting into trouble?

Here is what I tell students when I run sessions on this.

## The tools that actually help

The useful applications are unglamorous. None of them produce a finished piece
of work, which is exactly why they help.

**Explaining something until it lands.** If a lecturer's explanation did not
work for you, a language model will happily explain the same concept five
different ways, at whatever level you ask for. "Explain normalisation like I
have never seen a database" and then "now explain it like I am sitting the exam
next week" produce genuinely different, genuinely useful answers. This is the
single highest-value use and almost nobody talks about it.

**Generating practice questions.** Paste in your syllabus unit and ask for
twenty short questions, then answer them without looking. This works well
because you are still doing the thinking — the tool is only producing the
prompts.

**Attacking your own argument.** Paste in your draft thesis statement and ask
what a hostile examiner would say. The output is often blunt in a way that
supervisors are too polite to be, and you can act on it before submission
rather than after.

**Structuring a literature pile.** If you have thirty papers and no idea how
they relate, an assistant is good at proposing groupings. It is not good at
telling you which papers matter. That judgement is the part you are at
university to develop.

## What to be careful with

**Fabricated citations are the big one.** Every current assistant invents
references. Not occasionally — routinely. It will produce a citation with a real
journal name, plausible author names, a sensible-looking year and page range,
for a paper that has never existed. This is the most common way students get
caught, and it is entirely avoidable: if you have not opened the paper, do not
cite it.

**Confident wrong answers.** A model produces an incorrect explanation in
exactly the same tone as a correct one. There is no hesitation, no hedging, no
signal. For a subject you already understand, you will catch it. For a subject
you are learning, you will not — which is precisely when you are using it.

**Anything with real data in it.** Do not paste personal information, unpublished
research data, or anything covered by a confidentiality agreement into a
consumer AI tool. Assume it leaves your control the moment you press send.

## Where the academic honesty line sits

This is the question students actually want answered, and most guidance dodges
it.

The workable test is about who did the thinking. If you asked a tool to explain
recursion until you understood it, and then wrote your own answer, you learned
recursion — that is tutoring. If you asked it to write your assignment on
recursion and submitted that, you learned nothing, and the exam will find out
in a few weeks anyway.

The practical problem in Nepal right now is that institutional policies are
still being written. Some departments have clear rules, many do not, and "nobody
told me it was banned" has not worked as a defence anywhere in the world. So:

1. Ask your department for its policy, in writing.
2. If there is no policy, ask your supervisor directly what they consider
   acceptable, and keep the reply.
3. When in doubt, disclose what you used. Disclosure has never turned an
   acceptable use into misconduct. Concealment has done the reverse.

## Nepal context

Two things make this different here.

Connectivity is uneven, so tools that need a constant connection are less
useful during load-shedding or in districts with patchy service than the
articles written in San Francisco assume. Download what you need when you have
bandwidth.

Free tiers matter more. Most students are not paying twenty dollars a month.
The free tiers of the major assistants are genuinely capable for study work, and
anyone telling you that you need a paid plan to revise for an exam is selling
something.

## What you should do

Pick one assistant. Learn to give it proper context — your syllabus, your level,
what you have already tried. Use it to understand things, generate practice, and
stress-test your own work. Verify every factual claim and every citation
independently. Get your department's policy in writing.

That is the whole method. It is less exciting than the tool lists, and it is the
part that survives into your working life.

## Arjun's take

The students who get the most out of these tools are the ones who already study
well. AI amplifies an existing method; it does not supply one. If your revision
strategy is currently "read the notes the night before", adding an AI assistant
produces a faster version of a strategy that was not working.

I cover this properly in student sessions — including live examples of an
assistant confidently getting something wrong, which tends to land better than
being told it happens. If your college wants a session on it, that is
