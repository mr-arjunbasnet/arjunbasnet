Not everything should be automated, and the most enthusiastic candidate is rarely the right one. These seven come up repeatedly because they share three properties: high frequency, rules that can be written down, and a natural point where a person checks the output before it matters.

## 1. Quotation generation

The strongest candidate in most businesses. Structured inputs, deterministic pricing rules, high frequency, and a natural approval step before anything reaches the client.

The gain is not only time. Turnaround moves from next working day to within the hour, which affects win rates independently of what you charge.

The work is mostly in extracting the pricing rules, which usually live partly in a document and partly in one experienced person's head.

## 2. Recurring audits and reports

Anything performed on a schedule against the same checks, producing the same format. SEO audits, compliance checks, inventory reconciliation, weekly performance summaries.

These are ideal because the definition of correct is stable. Across the audit workflows I have built, a ten-hour manual cycle compressing to roughly two is typical — and the remaining two hours are spent interpreting results rather than gathering them.

## 3. Document processing

Invoices, receipts, purchase orders, application forms. Anything where a person currently reads a document and re-types its contents into a system.

This is where language models genuinely outperform older approaches, because they handle layout variation that rigid template extraction cannot. The important design decision is confidence thresholds: high-confidence extractions proceed, uncertain ones route to a person. Without that, you get confident errors, which are worse than obvious ones.

## 4. Lead routing and acknowledgement

An enquiry arrives. It gets classified, assigned to the right person, acknowledged with a real response rather than silence, and logged.

Every step is mechanical, and the cost of doing it badly is high — an enquiry sitting unacknowledged in a shared inbox over a weekend is a lost deal that nobody records as lost.

This one is often the cheapest to build and the fastest to show a return.

## 5. Onboarding sequences

A new client or employee triggers a predictable sequence: accounts created, documents sent, access granted, checklists started, reminders scheduled.

Done manually, steps get skipped inconsistently, and the ones that get skipped are usually the ones nobody notices until they matter. Automating this improves reliability more than it saves time, which is a legitimate reason to do it.

## 6. Content repurposing

One source asset — a case study, a recorded talk, a long article — reformatted into the shapes each channel needs.

Worth being clear about what this is and is not. It works well for reformatting and summarising existing material where a person reviews the output. It works badly as a content factory generating material nobody wrote, which produces volume that dilutes a site rather than strengthening it.

## 7. Data synchronisation between systems

Two systems holding overlapping data that someone keeps aligned by hand. A CRM and an accounting package. A booking system and a calendar. A spreadsheet somebody maintains as the real source of truth.

The manual version is not just slow, it is unreliable, because the sync only happens when someone remembers. Automating it removes a whole category of "the numbers do not match" problems.

## What does not belong on this list

Anything requiring negotiation or judgement about a person. Anything where the rules have never been written down — document first, automate second. Anything built on data that has never been cleaned, because automating a broken process only produces bad output faster.

And anything that changes every few months for good business reasons. Automation encodes a process; a process still in motion means rework that eats the saving.

## How to choose between them

Ask the person who performs each task — not their manager — how long it takes and how often. The answers differ more than you would expect, and the person doing the work is the one who is right.

Then pick the largest number of hours per week, not the most interesting problem. The most impressive-sounding automation is frequently not the one that pays for itself.

Build that one end to end, run it alongside the manual process for a week or two so the exceptions surface, and only then scope the second. That way the second is scoped against evidence rather than a projection.
